\title{The benchmark programme of \texttt{AdGIA}:\\
       a hierarchy of forward tests with \texttt{sl3dpp} and \texttt{pyslfp}}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This note is the write-up of the benchmarks of \code{AdGIA}, organised
around the cross-code programme with the two
spectral codes \code{sl3dpp} and \code{pyslfp}. The programme climbs a
hierarchy: elastic spherical models first (non-gravitating, then
gravitating; solid, then fluid--solid), then viscoelastic loading without
the sea-level equation, and only then sea level, so that a disagreement is
found on the lowest rung able to show it. Part~\ref{part:programme} sets out
the programme, its tiers, and the conventions every tier
shares: one model definition, one exchange format, tiered tolerances, and
the rules at degrees zero and one. Parts~\ref{part:elastic}--\ref{part:sea}
follow the hierarchy. Each tier has a section with a status line and
a fixed shape (the problem, the compared quantities, the references and the
legs, the results). The internal benchmark families
of \path{benchmarks/} --- benchmarks of a different kind, against exact or
independent references --- are placed where they belong in the hierarchy,
with their conclusions summarised; their detailed measurements stay in
\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, which this note cites and does not repeat. The
first tier, E1 (non-gravitating elastic, solid
spherical models), has its exact reference and both spectral legs in place
and gated, and its finite-element leg, \code{AdGIA}'s own, measured;
the second, E2 (gravitating elastic, solid and fluid--solid
models), has its machinery in place and measurements on six models;
and the first viscoelastic rung, V1 (Maxwell load Love-number histories of
gravitating models), has all three legs in place and measurements on
three models. Within E2 the note sets out why Dahlen's reduction of a static fluid leaves
degree zero undetermined, what fixes it, and why the general
three-dimensional answer is the Maxwell relaxation of the fluid.
\end{abstract}

\section{Why a hierarchy}
\label{sec:why}

A forward calculation of glacial isostatic adjustment stacks several
layers: the elastic response of a layered body; gravity and its
perturbation; fluid regions; the rheology and its time stepping; lateral
structure; and the sea-level equation, with its shorelines and its
rotational feedback. Two codes that disagree on the complete problem may
disagree in any of these, and a comparison made only at the top says how
large the spread is, not where it comes from. The published GIA benchmarks
show both the value and the limit of comparisons made high in the stack.
Spada et al.\ \cite{Spada2011} compared eight codes on one incompressible
Maxwell model, largely in the Laplace domain, and deliberately left out
compressibility and the sea-level equation. Martinec et al.\
\cite{Martinec2018} compared ten codes on the sea-level equation, on one
incompressible, non-rotating radial model; the codes agreed to about
1.5\,\%, and spatial discretisation and Gibbs behaviour at the load margins
were named as the main sources of the differences.

This programme goes the other way. It breaks the problem down as far as it
goes and compares the codes at every rung, so that each new ingredient is
tested on top of rungs already known to agree. Its principles:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Hierarchy, not headline.} Elastic before viscoelastic before
  sea level; non-gravitating before gravitating; simple spherical models
  before complex ones; loading without the sea-level equation before
  loading with it.
\item \emph{A practical target.} A GIA code must give stable, reliable
  results relative to \emph{observational} errors. Agreement to machine
  precision is not the goal, although understanding why two codes differ
  is valuable, and where two codes solve the identical discrete problem
  their agreement is expected at that level and is used as a gate.
\item \emph{Exact references where they exist.} The non-gravitating
  piecewise-homogeneous models have closed-form solutions, which is why the
  hierarchy starts there. Semi-analytical references (modal sums, inverse
  Laplace routes such as AAA with Talbot, or Gaver--Stehfest) are useful but
  secondary; some are fragile, particularly those that need an inverse
  Laplace transform.
\item \emph{Two modes for \code{AdGIA}.} Low-resolution runs that fit on a
  laptop are the default gate; server campaigns push the resolution.
  The benchmarks are not part of continuous integration, which holds each
  code's own tests.
\end{itemize}

\section{Two kinds of benchmark}
\label{sec:kinds}

The cross-code tiers set the codes against one another and, where one
exists, against an exact solution, always on spherical models and always
through a small set of numbers. Beside them, \path{benchmarks/} holds
\code{AdGIA}'s \emph{internal families}: Love numbers against
\code{pyslfp}, the relabelling identities, the interface-shift derivatives,
the two-dimensional disc, the manufactured solutions, the viscoelastic box,
sphere, stepping and Love-history sub-families, and the sea-level
fingerprints. These test
against exact closed forms, against identities that hold by construction
(the discrete change of variables under a relabelling), against refinement,
or against \code{pyslfp} as an independent radial reference. They reach
what no cross-code tier can --- the aspherical machinery, the
formulation-against-formulation questions, the time integrators in
isolation --- and they stay. Parts~\ref{part:elastic}--\ref{part:sea} place
each family at the rung where it belongs and state what it has
established; \code{doc/benchmarks.tex} holds their
measurements, problems and figures.

\subsection{Models: one definition}
\label{sec:models}

The models are the spherical ladder of \path{benchmarks/common/models.py}:
planetmodel \code{LayeredIsotropicElastic} models in SI, each layer
constant or polynomial in radius for $\rho$, $v_P$ and $v_S$, fluid layers
with $v_S=0$. The values are listed in \code{doc/benchmarks.tex} (``The
models''). The rungs used or planned by the tiers:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}P{4.2cm}P{5.4cm}P{5.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
model & structure & tiers \\
\midrule
\code{homogeneous} & one solid layer & E1 (exact), E2, V \\
\code{homogeneous\_lithosphere} & the same body cut at 5700\,km, so that
the outer shell can stay elastic & E1 (exact: elastically the homogeneous
body, a welded interface with no contrast), V \\
\code{two\_solid} & two homogeneous solid layers, interface at 5000\,km &
E1 (exact), E2, V \\
\code{linear\_solid} & one solid layer, linear in radius & E2, V \\
\code{fluid\_core}, \code{aw\_core} & homogeneous fluid core (non-neutral),
and its neutrally stratified twin & E2, V \\
\code{inner\_core}, \code{stratified\_core}, \code{earth\_like},
\code{prem\_4}, \code{prem\_6} & inner cores, stratified cores, PREM-grade
structure & E2, V, S \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

One model object feeds every code. \code{pyslfp} takes the planetmodel
objects directly. \code{sl3dpp} reads tabulated decks, written from the same
objects by \path{benchmarks/common/decks.py}: the elastic deck format
$r,\rho,v_{PV},v_{SV},v_{PH},v_{SH},\eta$ in SI (radius first; repeated
radii mark internal boundaries; $\eta$ the anisotropy parameter, one for
the isotropic ladder), eight knots per layer, every value written to full
precision (\code{\%.17g}). The deck is read back with a not-a-knot cubic
spline, which reproduces a polynomial of degree at most three exactly; every
ladder layer is such a polynomial except \code{aw\_core}'s closed-form
$v_P$, so for every other model the deck \emph{is} the model to rounding;
\code{decks.roundtrip\_error} measures the agreement, so that a generation
script can state it. \code{AdGIA} meshes the objects themselves
(\path{benchmarks/common/make_case.py}, through planetmodel and gmsh), as
its internal families do.

The radial mesh is part of the discrete problem on the spectral side.
\code{pyslfp}'s default is its graded mesh (\code{graded\_mesh}: five GLL
nodes per element, fine at the surface and widening with depth, so that
every degree to $l_{\max}$ is resolved at least as the uniform rule would
resolve it, with a few hundred elements). The uniform rule for a given
$l_{\max}$, elements no wider than $0.1a/(l_{\max}+1)$, is shared by the two
spectral codes
(\code{planetmodel}'s \code{RadialMesh} and \code{sl3dpp}'s
\code{RadialMesh::Create}); the same deck read on the same rule is the
identical discrete problem in both.

\subsection{Harmonics and the numbers of one degree}
\label{sec:harmonics}

On a spherically symmetric model the response to a forcing of one
spherical harmonic $\Ylm$ separates as
\begin{equation}
  \vu = U(r)\,\Ylm\,\rhat + V(r)\,\gradone\Ylm,
  \qquad \phi = \phi(r)\,\Ylm,
  \label{eq:separation}
\end{equation}
with $\gradone$ the surface gradient on the unit sphere. The surface values
of $U$, $V$ and $\phi$ per unit forcing depend on the degree $l$ alone: not
on the order $m$, and not on how the harmonics are normalised, since forcing
and response carry the same harmonic. The codes use real orthonormal
harmonics ($\int_{S^2}\Ylm^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega=1$, Condon--Shortley phase).
The normalisation matters only where a code builds or analyses fields:
\code{AdGIA}'s finite-element legs load with a harmonic and analyse the
solution on the surface with the same harmonics
(\code{BoundaryHarmonicCoefficients}).

\subsection{Units}
\label{sec:units}

Everything exchanged is in SI. The codes solve in different internal units
--- \code{AdGIA} in the units of \code{models.scales} (length the outer
radius, density 5000\,kg\,m$^{-3}$, time chosen so that $G=1$),
\code{pyslfp} and \code{sl3dpp} in \code{planetmodel} \code{Scales} with
different time scales --- so each code converts its own output and the
comparison is anchored in SI.

\subsection{The exchange format}
\label{sec:exchange}

Each code reduces its answer to a small text file: provenance lines, named
scalars, and one table. \path{benchmarks/common/exchange.py} writes and
reads it, and the C++ drivers reproduce its layout by hand.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Leading \code{\#} lines are provenance, free text. By convention the
  first says what the file holds and one reads \code{code <name> commit
  <sha> date <iso-date>}.
\item Bare \code{name value...} lines are named scalars.
\item A \code{columns} line names the columns of the table that follows,
  one row per line, every value SI and written to round-trip precision
  (\code{\%.17g}, or shortest round-trip in C++).
\item \code{nan} marks an excluded entry (a degree the problem does not
  define).
\end{itemize}
The first lines of the analytic E1 reference for \code{homogeneous}, as
\path{reference.py} writes it (two long lines shortened here):
\begin{verbatim}
# E1 non-gravitating traction numbers: the exact piecewise-homogeneous ...
# code analytic commit 5f2a5a3 date 2026-10-21
# model homogeneous SI (m per Pa); NaN = excluded degree
radius 6371000
gravitating 0
columns l h_u l_u h_v l_v
0 -6.4713052310817677e-06 0 0 0
1 nan nan nan nan
2 -1.3348735500072317e-05 -1.9325902953678312e-06 -1.1595541772206983e-05 ...
\end{verbatim}
Files are named by tier, model and code (\path{e1_<model>_<code>.txt}). The
format is deliberately plain: the aim is to reduce each comparison to a few
numbers or a simple plot. A genuine 3-D field (the lateral-viscosity tier)
calls for netCDF instead.

\subsection{Comparison and the error measure}
\label{sec:measure}

A comparison reads two exchange files and never needs the codes that wrote
them. \path{benchmarks/elastic/nongrav/compare.py}, the first such script,
takes the first file as the reference, matches the degree column and the
shared columns by name, and reports for each channel the worst relative
difference over the degrees, measured against the \emph{largest reference
value of the degree} over the shared channels:
\begin{equation}
  e_c = \max_l
  \frac{\bigl|x_c^{\text{other}}(l)-x_c^{\text{ref}}(l)\bigr|}
       {\max_{c'}\bigl|x_{c'}^{\text{ref}}(l)\bigr|} .
  \label{eq:degreescale}
\end{equation}
A \code{nan} in either file excludes the entry, and \code{--tol} turns the
report into a pass/fail exit status. The degree scale keeps a channel that
passes through zero from dominating: on \code{homogeneous}, $l_u$ changes
sign between degrees 3 and 4 (\S\ref{sec:e1-closed}), and at degree 4 it is
$8\times10^{-4}$ of $h_u$, so a relative error on its own value would
measure nothing physical. The history tiers key their tables on the time
$t$ instead, with the degree carried in the column names (\code{hp2 lp2
kp2}, \dots; \S\ref{sec:v1}); there the maximum in front of
\eqref{eq:degreescale} runs over the times, and the maximum in the
denominator over the degree's channels \emph{and} over all times, so that a
degree's whole history is judged on one scale.

The comparison rules, recorded first in \code{sl3dpp}'s decision log, hold
throughout: compare in coefficient space or through grid-independent
functionals, never point-wise on matched grids; compare gauge-invariant
quantities only; anchor in SI.

\subsection{Tolerances in tiers}
\label{sec:tolerances}

What counts as agreement depends on what is being compared:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{The identical discrete problem} --- \code{pyslfp} and
  \code{sl3dpp} on the same deck and mesh rule. Agreement is expected near
  machine precision and is a gate: $10^{-10}$ of the degree scale.
\item \emph{An exact reference against a spectral code} --- the code's own
  discretisation error, spectrally small and dependent on the mesh rule;
  about $10^{-11}$--$10^{-10}$ in E1 (\S\ref{sec:e1-results}).
\item \emph{A finite-element code against a reference} --- the
  discretisation level, established by refinement in $h$ and in order:
  judged by the levels and by convergence under refinement, not by a fixed
  threshold.
\end{enumerate}
The physical verdict is set by observational error: the codes must be
reliable against what can be observed, not against machine epsilon.

\subsection{Degrees zero and one}
\label{sec:degrees01}

\paragraph{Degree zero.} \code{pyslfp} solves degree 0 with the full
compressible (Lagrangian) equations, fluid regions included as elastic
media with zero shear modulus. \code{sl3dpp} does the same: it reproduces \code{pyslfp}'s degree-0
traction numbers on solid models on the identical discrete problem
(\S\ref{sec:e1-results}), and its degree-0 load numbers on
\code{fluid\_core} on independent meshes (\S\ref{sec:e2-results}). In
\code{AdGIA}, Dahlen's reduction of a fluid region eliminates the fluid's
displacement and with it its bulk modulus, which leaves an arbitrary
function at degree 0 (\S\ref{sec:e2-degree0}): the Dahlen path differs
from the others there by design, and is not benchmarked at $l=0$ except to
show the difference. The Maxwell-core treatment (\code{SetFluid}'s Maxwell
relaxation) keeps the compressible fluid and is the route benchmarked at
$l=0$. On solid models none of this arises. The programme's position,
argued in \S\ref{sec:e2-degree0}: on hydrostatic models Dahlen's
reduction closed by the level border (\code{doc/dahlen\_closure.tex};
\code{-level-border}, the default of the love-number family) is the
static route at every degree, degree 0 included; the Maxwell relaxation
is the general dissipative route, the one for non-hydrostatic and
non-neutral fluids; and the spectral codes' Lagrangian treatment is the
reference at degree 0.

\paragraph{Degree one.} Without gravity, a spheroidal surface traction of
degree 1 has a net force, which a free body cannot balance; the
non-gravitating tiers exclude the degree and write \code{nan}
(\S\ref{sec:e1-solvability}). With gravity, degree-1 quantities are
compared in the frame of the centre of mass of the body and its load, in
which the external potential has no degree-one part; \code{AdGIA}'s
Love-number driver shifts its solution into that frame
(\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, ``Degree one: the centre-of-mass frame''), and
E2 gives the first cross-code check of the conventions: on
\code{two\_solid} and \code{fluid\_core} the degree-1 numbers agree with
\code{pyslfp}'s at the discretisation level, with $k'_1=-1$ to rounding in
both codes (\S\ref{sec:e2-results}). V1 confirms them for the viscoelastic
histories: $k'_1=-1$ to rounding at every time in all three codes, and the
degree-1 histories agree with \code{pyslfp}'s to
$4\times10^{-4}$--$1.5\times10^{-2}$ at order 2 and
$2\times10^{-5}$--$6\times10^{-4}$ at order 3 (\S\ref{sec:v1}). The rotational (toroidal, rigid-rotation) part of degree 1 is
more a convention than a frame and is documented by each code separately;
where it matters, the comparison uses quantities invariant under rigid
motions, such as changes of length between points. Comparisons at
$l\ne1$ are not affected.

\subsection{Where things live}
\label{sec:where}

Everything cross-code --- models, decks, exchange, comparison scripts,
results and this note --- lives in \code{AdGIA}; \code{sl3dpp} and
\code{pyslfp} keep their own internal tests. The tiers have directories of
\path{benchmarks/}: \path{elastic/nongrav/} (E1) and
\path{elastic/grav/} (E2), and the spherical viscoelastic tier (V1)
lives in \path{viscoelastic/love/}, the home of the Love-history
family, beside the other family directories. The shared pieces are in \path{benchmarks/common/}
(\code{models.py}, \code{decks.py}, \code{exchange.py}, \code{analytic.py},
\code{outputs.py}). \code{sl3dpp}'s drivers live in the \code{sl3dpp}
repository, built with \code{-DSL3DPP\_BUILD\_BENCHMARKS=ON}, and are used
from here; scripts that call \code{pyslfp} run from the \code{pyslfp}
checkout, which owns its environment. Every script refuses to write into
the source tree (\path{common/outputs.py}).

\section{Tier E1: non-gravitating elastic, solid models}
\label{sec:e1}

\status{The exact reference (\path{common/analytic.py})
is done. The \code{pyslfp} and \code{sl3dpp} legs are done and gated: on the
identical discrete problem against each other, and against the exact
reference (\S\ref{sec:e1-results}). \code{AdGIA}'s finite-element leg,
\code{traction\_benchmark}, is implemented and measured on
\code{two\_solid} at two resolutions (\S\ref{sec:e1-fe}).}

\subsection{The problem}
\label{sec:e1-problem}

A solid ball $B$ of radius $a$, spherically layered, with Lam\'e moduli
$\lambda(r),\mu(r)$, no gravity, no pre-stress and no body force, is held
in static equilibrium by a prescribed traction $\vt$ on its surface:
\begin{equation}
  \nabla\cdot\bsig = \mathbf{0}\ \text{in } B,\qquad
  \bsig\cdot\rhat = \vt\ \text{on } r=a,\qquad
  \bsig = \lambda(\nabla\cdot\vu)\,\mathbf{I}
        + \mu\bigl(\nabla\vu+\nabla\vu^{T}\bigr),
  \label{eq:e1-problem}
\end{equation}
with displacement and traction continuous across every interface (welded).
In the separation \eqref{eq:separation}, the traction on a sphere is
$\bsig\cdot\rhat = T_U\,Y\,\rhat + T_V\,\gradone Y$, with
\begin{equation}
  T_U = \lambda\Bigl(U' + \frac{2U - l(l+1)V}{r}\Bigr) + 2\mu U',
  \qquad
  T_V = \mu\Bigl(V' + \frac{U-V}{r}\Bigr),
  \label{eq:tractions}
\end{equation}
so that at every degree the problem is a two-point boundary-value problem
for $(U,V)$, regular at the centre, with $(U,V,T_U,T_V)$ continuous at the
interfaces and $(T_U,T_V)$ given at the surface. At degree 0, $\gradone Y$
vanishes and only $U$ and $T_U$ remain. This is the bottom rung of the
hierarchy: it tests the elastic operator, the layering and the surface
analysis with nothing else switched on, and on the piecewise-homogeneous
ladder models it has an exact solution.

\subsection{The traction numbers}
\label{sec:e1-numbers}

The forcings are unit tractions, per unit coefficient of the harmonic:
\begin{align}
  \text{normal (pressing):}&\quad \vt = -Y\,\rhat, &
    T_U(a)&=-1,\ T_V(a)=0; \notag\\
  \text{tangential:}&\quad \vt = -\gradone Y, &
    T_U(a)&=0,\ T_V(a)=-1.
\end{align}
The \emph{traction numbers} are the surface values of the response:
\begin{align}
  h_u &= U(a),\quad l_u = V(a) && \text{under the normal traction}; \notag\\
  h_v &= U(a),\quad l_v = V(a) && \text{under the tangential traction}.
\end{align}
They are displacements per unit traction, metres per pascal, and by
\S\ref{sec:harmonics} independent of $m$ and of the harmonics'
normalisation. At degree 0 the tangential channel does not exist and
$l_u=h_v=l_v=0$.

The names are those of the generalised Love numbers, whose ``force''
(subscript $u$) and tangential ($v$) channels are the responses to a unit
surface \emph{density}. A surface density $\sigma$ on the gravitating problem
presses on the surface with the traction $g\sigma$, and \code{pyslfp}'s load
force is built as exactly $g$ times its unit traction (likewise the
tangential forcing), so on the gravitating problem the traction numbers are
the corresponding generalised Love numbers divided by the surface gravity
$g$. Both spectral codes test this tie to $10^{-13}$. The traction numbers
are therefore the channels of the generalised Love numbers that survive
when gravity is switched off, which is what lets one forcing serve E1 and
E2: \code{pyslfp}'s \code{traction\_numbers(model, lmax,
gravitating=True)} solves the full problem under the same unit tractions.

\subsection{Solvability, degree one and degree zero}
\label{sec:e1-solvability}

In weak form, \eqref{eq:e1-problem} asks for $\vu$ with
\begin{equation}
  \int_B \bsig(\vu):\nabla\vw\dV = \int_{\partial B}\vt\cdot\vw\dS
  \quad\text{for every } \vw .
\end{equation}
The left side vanishes for every rigid motion $\vw$ (a translation or an
infinitesimal rotation), so a solution exists only if the traction does no
work on the rigid motions, and it is then unique up to one. A translation
$\mathbf{c}$ is spheroidal of degree 1:
$\mathbf{c} = (\mathbf{c}\cdot\rhat)\,\rhat + \gradone(\mathbf{c}\cdot\rhat)$
with $\mathbf{c}\cdot\rhat$ a degree-1 harmonic. A rotation is toroidal.
Hence, by orthogonality of the harmonics:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item at $l\ne1$, every spheroidal traction is orthogonal to every rigid
  motion; the problem is solvable, and since rigid motions have no
  spheroidal component of degree $l\ne1$, $U$ and $V$ are unique;
\item at $l=1$, the normal traction $-Y_{1m}\rhat$ does the work
  $-a^2\int Y_{1m}(\mathbf{c}\cdot\rhat)\,\mathrm{d}\Omega\ne0$ on the
  translation along its axis, and the tangential one $-\gradone Y_{1m}$
  twice that: both carry a net force, and there is no static solution.
\end{itemize}
Degree 1 is therefore excluded from E1 and written \code{nan} by every code;
it returns, with gravity, in E2. The exclusion is visible in the closed
form of \S\ref{sec:e1-closed}, which has a pole at $l=1$.

At degree 0 the displacement is radial, and on a homogeneous body the
solution is uniform compression, $\vu = A\vx$, $\bsig = 3\kappa A\,\mathbf
I$ with $\kappa=\lambda+\tfrac23\mu$. A unit pressing traction gives
$3\kappa A=-1$, so
\begin{equation}
  h_u(0) = -\frac{a}{3\kappa},
  \label{eq:degree0}
\end{equation}
which is $-6.4713\times10^{-6}$\,m\,Pa$^{-1}$ on \code{homogeneous}
($\kappa = 3.2817\times10^{11}$\,Pa). Both spectral codes test this closed
form to $10^{-12}$.

\subsection{Reciprocity}
\label{sec:e1-reciprocity}

The elastic operator is self-adjoint, so for two tractions $\vt_1,\vt_2$
with responses $\vu_1,\vu_2$ Betti's reciprocal theorem gives
$\int_{\partial B}\vt_1\cdot\vu_2\dS = \int_{\partial B}\vt_2\cdot\vu_1\dS$.
With $\vt_1=-Y\rhat$ and $\vt_2=-\gradone Y$ at one degree,
\begin{equation}
  -a^2 h_v\!\int Y^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega
  = -a^2 l_u\!\int |\gradone Y|^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega
  = -a^2\, l(l+1)\, l_u\!\int Y^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega ,
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  h_v = l(l+1)\,l_u ,
  \label{eq:reciprocity}
\end{equation}
exactly, whatever the layering. A symmetric Galerkin discretisation whose
loads and surface read-outs are the same functionals keeps it exactly, so in
the spectral codes it holds to solver precision, and the residual
$|h_v - l(l+1)l_u|/\max(|h_v|,|l(l+1)l_u|)$ is reported by every leg as a
free internal check. It also halves what has to be compared: $h_v$ carries
no information that $l_u$ does not.

\subsection{The exact reference}
\label{sec:e1-exact}

\path{benchmarks/common/analytic.py} solves E1 exactly on models whose
layers are solid and homogeneous (it samples $\rho$, $v_P$, $v_S$ in each
layer and refuses a layer that is not constant to rounding). The
construction follows.

\paragraph{Harmonic potentials.}
In a homogeneous layer, Navier's equation
$(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vu)+\mu\nabla^2\vu=\mathbf 0$ has, at
each degree, solutions built from the two harmonic functions
$\chi = r^{n}Y$ with $n=l$ (regular at the centre) or $n=-(l+1)$
(singular); both have $n(n+1)=l(l+1)$, and $\chi$ is homogeneous of degree
$n$, so $(\vx\cdot\nabla)\chi = n\chi$.

\paragraph{Family A: the gradient solution.} $\vu=\nabla\chi$ is
divergence-free and harmonic, so it satisfies Navier's equation. In the
separation,
\begin{equation}
  U = n\,r^{n-1},\qquad V = r^{n-1}.
\end{equation}
Its stress is $2\mu\nabla\nabla\chi$, and since $\nabla\chi$ is homogeneous
of degree $n-1$, $\bsig\cdot\rhat = (2\mu/r)(\vx\cdot\nabla)\nabla\chi =
(2\mu(n-1)/r)\nabla\chi$:
\begin{equation}
  T_U = 2\mu\,n(n-1)\,r^{n-2},\qquad T_V = 2\mu\,(n-1)\,r^{n-2}.
  \label{eq:familyA}
\end{equation}
The same follows from \eqref{eq:tractions}: $T_U = 2\mu U'$ since the
divergence vanishes, and $T_V = \mu\bigl((n-1) - 1 + n\bigr)r^{n-2}$. A
check: at $n=1$ the solution is a uniform translation, and both tractions
vanish.

\paragraph{Family B: the Kelvin combination.} Take
$\vu = c_1\,r^2\nabla\chi + c_2\,\vx\,\chi$. The identities
\begin{equation}
  \nabla\cdot(r^2\nabla\chi) = 2n\chi,\quad
  \nabla\cdot(\vx\chi) = (n+3)\chi,\quad
  \nabla^2(r^2\nabla\chi) = (4n+2)\nabla\chi,\quad
  \nabla^2(\vx\chi) = 2\nabla\chi
\end{equation}
(the third from $\nabla^2(r^2\mathbf f) = 6\mathbf f + 4(\vx\cdot\nabla)
\mathbf f$ for harmonic $\mathbf f$, with $\mathbf f=\nabla\chi$ homogeneous
of degree $n-1$) reduce Navier's operator to
\begin{equation}
  \Bigl\{c_1\bigl[2n(\lambda+\mu)+(4n+2)\mu\bigr]
       + c_2\bigl[(n+3)(\lambda+\mu)+2\mu\bigr]\Bigr\}\nabla\chi ,
\end{equation}
which vanishes identically for
\begin{equation}
  c_1 = (n+3)(\lambda+\mu)+2\mu = (n+3)\lambda+(n+5)\mu,
  \qquad
  c_2 = -\bigl[2n(\lambda+\mu)+(4n+2)\mu\bigr] = -2\bigl[n\lambda+(3n+1)\mu\bigr],
  \label{eq:kelvin}
\end{equation}
the second solution of the classical theory \cite{Love1927}, written
without the division by $c_1$ that the usual normalisation makes. The
division-free form matters: $c_1$ can vanish on the singular side, at
$n = -3-2\mu/(\lambda+\mu)$, for instance at $l=3$ ($n=-4$) in a Poisson
solid ($\lambda=\mu$). The solution is then $c_2\,\vx\chi$, still a solution
and still independent of family A. The two coefficients never vanish
together: that requires $(\lambda+\mu)/\mu\in\{-1,-\tfrac43\}$, excluded
for a stable solid, where $\lambda+\mu=\kappa+\mu/3>0$. In the separation,
\begin{equation}
  U = (c_1 n + c_2)\,r^{n+1},\qquad V = c_1\,r^{n+1},\qquad
  \nabla\cdot\vu = \bigl(2nc_1+(n+3)c_2\bigr)\,r^{n}\,Y,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  T_U = \Bigl[\lambda\bigl(2nc_1+(n+3)c_2\bigr)
              + 2\mu(n+1)(c_1n+c_2)\Bigr]r^{n},
  \qquad
  T_V = \mu\,(2nc_1+c_2)\,r^{n}.
  \label{eq:familyB}
\end{equation}
For the regular solution ($n=l$) these simplify to
$c_1l+c_2=(l+1)[l\lambda+(l-2)\mu]$,
$\nabla\cdot\vu = -2(l+1)(2l+3)\mu\,r^lY$,
$T_U=2\mu(l+1)[(l^2-l-3)\lambda+(l^2-l-2)\mu]\,r^l$ and
$T_V=2\mu[l(l+2)\lambda+(l^2+2l-1)\mu]\,r^l$: family A carries no
dilatation, family B all of it.

The four solutions of degree $l\ge2$ have $U\propto r^{l-1}$, $r^{l+1}$
(regular) and $r^{-l-2}$, $r^{-l}$ (singular), four distinct powers, so they
are independent for every admissible pair of moduli. These powers are the
eigenvalues, $l-1$, $l+1$, $-l-2$, $-l$, of the Euler-type radial system
that the viscoelastic sphere sub-family derives independently
(\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, ``Sphere benchmarks: problems and exact
references''), which is the basis of the cross-check below. The Navier
equation, the four tractions \eqref{eq:familyA}, \eqref{eq:familyB} and the
degree-0 pair below were checked symbolically in Cartesian coordinates
(sympy) for concrete regular and singular harmonics of degrees 2 and 3
while this note was written.

\paragraph{Degree zero.} With $\gradone Y_{00}=0$ only $U$ survives, and
two of the four solutions collapse. Family A at $n=0$ is the gradient of a
constant, zero; family B at $n=-1$ has $c_1n+c_2=0$, so $U=0$. What remains
is family B at $n=0$, $U\propto r$, and family A at $n=-1$, $U\propto
r^{-2}$:
\begin{equation}
  U = r:\ \ T_U = 3\lambda+2\mu = 3\kappa;
  \qquad
  U = r^{-2}:\ \ T_U = -4\mu\,r^{-3}.
\end{equation}
The first is uniform compression, and with $T_U(a)=-1$ it gives
\eqref{eq:degree0}; the second is the divergence-free field of a
centre of dilatation.

\paragraph{The welded layer system.} Let the layers be $[r_{j-1},r_j]$,
$j=1,\dots,N$, with $r_0=0$ and $r_N=a$, and write the state
$\mathbf s=(U,V,T_U,T_V)^T$ in layer $j$ as $\mathbf s=\mathsf B_j(r)\,
\mathbf c_j$, the columns of $\mathsf B_j$ being the four solutions with
the layer's moduli. Regularity keeps only the two regular columns in the
innermost layer. The interface and surface conditions are
\begin{equation}
  \mathsf B_j(r_j)\,\mathbf c_j = \mathsf B_{j+1}(r_j)\,\mathbf c_{j+1}
  \quad (j=1,\dots,N-1),
  \qquad
  \bigl[\mathsf B_N(a)\,\mathbf c_N\bigr]_{T_U,T_V} = (-1,0)\ \text{or}\ (0,-1),
\end{equation}
$4(N-1)+2$ equations for $2+4(N-1)$ unknowns (at degree 0, with the state
$(U,T_U)$, $2(N-1)+1$ for $1+2(N-1)$). The traction numbers are the
$(U,V)$ rows of $\mathsf B_N(a)\,\mathbf c_N$. The state is propagated
across layer $j$ by the matrix $\mathsf B_j(r_j)\,\mathsf B_j(r_{j-1})^{-1}$,
which in the Euler form is $(r_j/r_{j-1})^{M}$; \code{analytic.py} does not
form these products but assembles and solves the block-bidiagonal system in
all the coefficients at once, so growing and decaying powers are never
multiplied together.

\paragraph{Layer-scaled radii.} In layer $j$ the basis is evaluated at
$x=r/r_j$, the radius scaled by the layer's top. Each column changes by a
constant factor $r_j^{p}$, absorbed into $\mathbf c_j$, and the traction
rows, which carry one radial derivative, are divided by $r_j$ to stay
physical. This is necessary, not cosmetic: in metres, $r^{l}$ overflows
double precision near $l\approx45$ at $r\approx6.4\times10^6$\,m. Scaled,
the regular columns are of order one at the top of their layer and smaller
below it, and the singular columns grow towards the layer's bottom like
$(r_j/r_{j-1})^{l+2}$, a factor of about $4\times10^3$ at $l=32$ across the
upper layer of \code{two\_solid}. The reference is used to $l=32$ here; its
reciprocity residual stays at $\sim10^{-14}$ there
(\S\ref{sec:e1-results}).

\paragraph{The homogeneous sphere in closed form.}
\label{sec:e1-closed}
On one homogeneous layer the system is $2\times2$, and solving it
(symbolically) gives, for $l\ge2$, with
$\Delta_l = (2l^2+4l+3)\lambda + 2(l^2+l+1)\mu$,
\begin{align}
  h_u &= -\frac{a\,\bigl[\,l(2l+1)\lambda + 2(2l^2-1)\mu\,\bigr]}{2\mu(l-1)\,\Delta_l},
  &
  l_u &= -\frac{a\,\bigl[\,3\lambda - 2(l-2)\mu\,\bigr]}{2\mu(l-1)\,\Delta_l},
  \notag\\
  h_v &= l(l+1)\,l_u,
  &
  l_v &= -\frac{a\,\bigl[\,(2l^2+l+3)\lambda + 2(2l^2-l+1)\mu\,\bigr]}{2\mu(l-1)\,\Delta_l}.
  \label{eq:closed}
\end{align}
These agree with \code{analytic.py} on \code{homogeneous} to
$1.6\times10^{-14}$ of the degree scale for $l=2,\dots,32$. They carry the
structure described above and three independent checks:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Reciprocity} \eqref{eq:reciprocity} holds identically, and
  the factor $l-1$ in the denominator is the degree-1 pole of
  \S\ref{sec:e1-solvability}.
\item \emph{The half-space limit.} At high degree, $h_u$ and $l_v$ both
  tend to $-a(\lambda+2\mu)/\bigl(2\mu(\lambda+\mu)\,l\bigr) =
  -(1-\nu)a/(\mu l)$, the compliance $(1-\nu)/(\mu k)$ of an elastic
  half-space at wavenumber $k=l/a$ ($\nu$ Poisson's ratio); on
  \code{homogeneous} at $l=32$ the limit differs from $h_u$ by about
  1\,\%, as an $O(1/l)$ correction should.
\item \emph{Kelvin's rigid sphere.} In the incompressible limit
  $\lambda\to\infty$, $h_u(2)\to -5a/(19\mu)$. In an incompressible
  homogeneous body a harmonic tidal potential $W=(r/a)^2W_aY$ acts only
  through the pressure: absorbing $\rho W$ into it turns the body force into
  a surface traction $\rho W_a Y\rhat$ that pulls. The surface uplift is then
  $5\rho W_a a/(19\mu)$, and $h_2 = gU(a)/W_a = 5\rho g a/(19\mu)$, the
  large-rigidity limit of Kelvin's $h_2 = \tfrac52/(1+19\mu/(2\rho g a))$
  \cite{Love1927}.
\end{itemize}
The formula also shows why the degree-scale measure of
\S\ref{sec:measure} is needed: $l_u$ changes sign at
$l = 2+3\lambda/(2\mu)$, which is $3.96$ on \code{homogeneous}
($\lambda/\mu=1.306$).

\paragraph{A second exact reference.} The viscoelastic sphere sub-family
computes the same per-degree elastic response by a different route: the
Euler system $r\,\mathrm d\mathbf Y/\mathrm dr = M\mathbf Y$ for
$\mathbf Y=(U,V,rT_U,rT_V)$, its regular eigenvectors in the central layer,
and the matrix-exponential propagator $(r_j/r_{j-1})^M$ across the others
(\path{benchmarks/viscoelastic/sphere/reference.py}, derived with sympy by
\path{derive_euler.py}). Its Euler matrix, with real moduli and propagated
as its transfer function does, gives on the E1 ladder models a $U(a),V(a)$
per unit pressure that agrees with \code{analytic.py}'s
$h_u,l_u$ to $3.8\times10^{-15}$ (\code{homogeneous}), $1.8\times10^{-12}$
(\code{homogeneous\_lithosphere}) and $2.5\times10^{-12}$
(\code{two\_solid}), relative to $\max(|h_u|,|l_u|)$ per degree, over
$l=2,\dots,32$. The two references share no code and are derived
differently (explicit potentials and a global solve; the Euler matrix and
propagator products), so E1's reference is itself checked.

\paragraph{What gravity changes.} The gravitating fixtures show the size of
the step to E2 under the same forcing (\code{pyslfp}, uniform
$l_{\max}=8$ rule). The ratio of the gravitating to the non-gravitating
$h_u$ is $1.43$ at degree 0 and $0.935$--$0.976$ over degrees 2--8 on

\subsection{The finite-element leg}
\label{sec:e1-fe}

\code{AdGIA}'s leg is the one E1 exists for. \code{traction\_benchmark}
(\path{benchmarks/elastic/nongrav/traction_benchmark.cpp}, built with the
other drivers) takes a case written by \code{make\_case.py} for a ladder
model: its mesh, $\kappa$ and $\mu$, nothing about the model set in the
driver, and a refusal if the case has a fluid region. It solves
\eqref{eq:e1-problem} with \code{LinearQuasiStaticTractionProblem} on the
body alone (the buffer shell of the gravitating cases is left out), with no
gravity and no potential and the rigid modes projected out in the solver.
For each degree, order 0, the surface traction is $-Y_{l0}\rhat$ and, for
$l\ge2$, $-\gradone Y_{l0}$, applied one at a time; the displacement is
analysed into radial and tangential harmonic coefficients on the surface
(\code{BoundaryHarmonicCoefficients}); and the $(l,0)$ coefficients,
converted to SI through the case's scales, are written as an exchange file,
degree 1 as \code{nan}. The driver also reports the reciprocity residual
and \code{spurious}, the largest unforced coefficient relative to the
forced ones. The machinery is not new: the viscoelastic sphere sub-family
solves the same free-body pressure problem on curved layered balls with the
same classes (\S\ref{sec:internal-visco}). What E1 adds is the shared models
in SI, the tangential channel, and a reference that the spectral codes are
held to as well.

The rung covers solid models and fluid--solid models. One family carries
both, so they are written here as one tier, solid models first; the fluid--solid models bring the one theoretical question of
the elastic tiers, the degree-0 response of a static fluid
(\S\ref{sec:e2-degree0}).

\subsection{The problem and the compared quantities}
\label{sec:e2-problem}

The full linearised, quasi-static, self-gravitating elastic response of
spherical models to surface loads and tidal potentials, the perturbation of
the potential solved inside and outside the body. The models: solid
(\code{homogeneous}, \code{two\_solid}, \code{linear\_solid}), then
fluid--solid (\code{fluid\_core}, \code{aw\_core}, \code{inner\_core},
\code{stratified\_core}, \code{earth\_like}, \code{prem\_4}). There is no
non-gravitating fluid--solid rung: a static fluid without gravity has no
restoring force. The gravity split is two-way, no gravity (E1) or full
gravity (E2); an intermediate variant (background gravity with the
potential perturbation dropped) is added only if an issue arises that it
would help isolate.

The compared quantities are the conventional Love numbers, which every code
computes: the load numbers $h'_l,l'_l,k'_l$ (Farrell's, referred to the
load's direct potential, geodetic sign) and the geodetic tidal numbers
$h_l,l_l,k_l$ (\code{nan} below degree 2), dimensionless, in the columns
\code{l hp lp kp hT lT kT}. Being dimensionless, they need no conversion to
SI. Degree 1 is in the centre-of-mass frame in every code, so $k'_1=-1$
identically; degree 0 is compared except on \code{AdGIA}'s Dahlen path with
a fluid region, where it is wrong by design and is shown, not benchmarked.
The gravitating traction numbers (the forcing of E1 with gravity on) are
compared between the spectral codes, where they isolate the effect of
gravity (\S\ref{sec:e1-results}).

\paragraph{The spectral pair.} Unlike the E1 gate, this is not the identical
discrete problem: the meshes are independent (graded against uniform rule)
and so are the model definitions (the planetmodel object against the deck).
Agreement at $5\times10^{-11}$ says that both codes are converged to that
level, which is tolerance tier (ii) between two codes. It includes degree 0
on \code{fluid\_core} ($h'_0$ to $2\times10^{-12}$), which verifies that
\code{sl3dpp} treats degree 0 with a fluid layer as \code{pyslfp} does. On
\code{inner\_core} and \code{stratified\_core}, whose layers are
polynomials so that the deck is the model to rounding (round trips 0 and
$2\times10^{-16}$), the pair agrees to $10^{-11}$, and \code{sl3dpp}'s
reciprocity residual is $4$--$8\times10^{-16}$ on the three variants.

\paragraph{\code{aw\_core}: the deck's own accuracy.} On \code{aw\_core}
the pair agrees to $10^{-11}$ at every degree $l\ge1$ and in every channel
but one: $h'_0$ differs by $8.2\times10^{-8}$. This is the deck, not the
codes. The core of \code{aw\_core} has the closed-form Adams--Williamson
$v_P$, the only field of the ladder that is not a polynomial
(\S\ref{sec:models}), so the eight-knot cubic deck reproduces the model
only to spline accuracy, a round trip of $9.5\times10^{-7}$ as
\path{decks.py} reports it. Written with sixteen knots per layer (round
trip $5.4\times10^{-8}$), the deck brings $h'_0$ to $2.0\times10^{-9}$ and
leaves $l\ge1$ unchanged. Degree 0 is the channel sensitive to the core's
compressibility profile, which is exactly what the deck samples. Above
degree 0 every code makes the static-fluid reduction --- the spectral
codes' fluid regions enter through the potential alone, as \code{AdGIA}'s
do through Dahlen's elimination (\S\ref{sec:e2-degree0}) --- with a
stratification term built from $\rho$ and $\mathrm{d}\rho/\mathrm{d}r$
only, so the bulk modulus, the one field the deck approximates, enters
only at degree 0, where the full compressible equations apply; the core's
density, a quadratic in radius, the deck reproduces exactly. (\code{AdGIA}
builds its mesh from the model directly and never sees the deck.) The
programme's position:
\code{pyslfp}, from the model object, and \code{sl3dpp}, from the deck,
agree to the deck's own accuracy; for \code{aw\_core} the deck is written
with more knots (\code{decks.py --knots}) when degree 0 is at stake, and the
round-trip error that \path{decks.py} reports is the number to quote with
the comparison.

\paragraph{\code{AdGIA} on a solid model.} On \code{two\_solid} the Dahlen
path agrees with \code{pyslfp} to about $10^{-3}$ in the well-resolved
channels ($l'$, $k'$ and the tidal numbers at low degree) and to
$10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$ in $h'$, the error growing with degree to
$2.2\times10^{-2}$ at degree 6, the resolution edge of $h=0.3$: the grade of
the Love-number family at this resolution (\S\ref{sec:internal-elastic}). At degree 1 the numbers agree to
$1.4\times10^{-3}$ with $k'_1=-1$ to rounding in both codes. This is a
cross-code check of the centre-of-mass frame conventions.

\paragraph{\code{AdGIA} on a fluid core.} On \code{fluid\_core} the Dahlen
path agrees with \code{pyslfp} at the same finite-element level for
$l\ge1$, while its degree 0 is wrong by design: $h'_0$ is about eleven times
the spectral value, and $k'_0=-5.35$ where mass conservation requires 0
(forty times the degree's scale). This is the arbitrary function of
\S\ref{sec:e2-degree0}, exhibited rather than benchmarked. The Maxwell-core
method agrees at every degree including 0 --- $h'_0$ to $4.5\times10^{-3}$,
and $k'_0=-7\times10^{-6}$, mass conserved to the discretisation level ---
at several times Dahlen's cost per solve. Its degree-1 $h'$ sits
$1.6\times10^{-2}$ from \code{pyslfp}'s, that is from the Dahlen (secular)
closure. That is not a frame or discretisation error: the Maxwell route's
discrete limit is the welded static solve, which on a core with
$N^2\neq0$ returns the welded point of the elastic-static band rather than
the secular closure (\code{doc/static\_fluid\_core.tex}, ``The band, the
secular state, and the Maxwell route''). Its degree-1 relaxation also stops
at an increment of about $10^{-2}$, the soft core--shell translation
(\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, ``The degree-1 adjudication''), but that is a
diagnostic of the stop, not the offset. The neutral \code{aw\_core} is the
model on which the methods are expected to coincide.

\paragraph{\code{AdGIA} on the fluid-core variants.} The pattern of
\code{fluid\_core} repeats on every variant. The Dahlen path agrees with
\code{pyslfp} at the finite-element level for $l\ge1$, the error growing
with degree to the resolution edge: on \code{aw\_core}, $h'_2$, $h'_3$ and
$h'_4$ at $7.3\times10^{-3}$, $1.4\times10^{-2}$ and $2.1\times10^{-2}$,
$l'$ and $k'$ at most $8.6\times10^{-3}$, tidal $h_2$ at
$1.0\times10^{-2}$; on \code{stratified\_core}, $h'_3$ and $h'_4$ at
$8.4\times10^{-3}$ and $1.6\times10^{-2}$, $l'$ at most $9.7\times10^{-3}$,
tidal $h_2$ at $5.1\times10^{-3}$; and $k'_1=-1$ to rounding throughout.
Its degree 0 is wrong by design: $h'_0$ is 4.0, 10.3 and 5.1 times the
spectral value on \code{aw\_core}, \code{inner\_core} and
\code{stratified\_core}, and $k'_0$ is $-2.20$, $-5.02$ and $-3.03$ where
mass conservation requires 0 --- the arbitrary function of
\S\ref{sec:e2-degree0} again. The Maxwell-core route is right at every
degree: $h'_0$ to $4.4$--$5.0\times10^{-3}$ and $k'_0\approx-7\times10^{-6}$
on all three, the other degrees at the Dahlen path's level (worst
$3.2$--$3.3\times10^{-2}$; $k'_2$ $5.5\times10^{-3}$, $1.4\times10^{-2}$
and $3.8\times10^{-3}$; tidal $h_2$ $1.0\times10^{-2}$, $1.5\times10^{-2}$
and $8.2\times10^{-3}$), at 14 to 25 times Dahlen's wall time here.

On \code{aw\_core} and \code{inner\_core} the Dahlen errors at degrees 3--6
coincide to $3\times10^{-4}$ or better (to $5\times10^{-5}$ at degrees 5 and
6), and $h'_6$ is $3.6\times10^{-2}$ on both; they differ only at degrees 1
and 2, by up to $4\times10^{-3}$ and $10^{-3}$. The two models share
\code{fluid\_core}'s mantle, and at $h=0.3$ the large errors at high degree
are the mantle's discretisation at the resolution edge, not the core's
($h'_6$ is $2.9\times10^{-2}$ on \code{stratified\_core}, whose mantle
differs). On \code{stratified\_core} the Dahlen path is unusually close at
degree 2: $h'_2$ to $7.4\times10^{-4}$ and $k'_2$ to $7.3\times10^{-6}$.

Degree 1 on the Maxwell path. The measured quantity is the Maxwell
route's degree-1 offset from \code{pyslfp}, that is from the Dahlen
(secular) closure: $h'_1$ sits $6.1\times10^{-4}$ from it on the neutral
\code{aw\_core}, against $1.5\times10^{-2}$ on \code{inner\_core},
$8.7\times10^{-3}$ on \code{stratified\_core} and $1.6\times10^{-2}$ on
\code{fluid\_core}. On \code{aw\_core} the two routes coincide, its Maxwell
and Dahlen degree-1 numbers both within $2\times10^{-3}$ of
\code{pyslfp}'s. The relaxation's stop increments at degree 1 are
diagnostics, quoted separately --- load and tide, $2.7\times10^{-3}$ and
$8.8\times10^{-4}$ on \code{aw\_core}, $1.1\times10^{-2}$ and
$1.1\times10^{-2}$ on \code{inner\_core}, $3.0\times10^{-4}$ and
$2.7\times10^{-3}$ on \code{stratified\_core} --- and they are not a
measure of the offset: \code{stratified\_core} stops at a small increment
and still sits $8.7\times10^{-3}$ off. The interpretation, the welded
discrete limit of the route against the secular closure, and the table of
$N^2$ across the ladder's cores (only the three stable cores are stably
stratified throughout) are in

Maxwell route''; ``Stably stratified cores on the ladder'').

\subsection{Degree zero with a fluid region}
\label{sec:e2-degree0}

\paragraph{Dahlen's static reduction.} Let the background be hydrostatic,
$\nabla p_0 = -\rho_0\nabla\Phi_0$, so that $p_0$ and $\rho_0$ are constant
on the level surfaces of $\Phi_0$ ($\nabla\Phi_0 = g\,\vn$, $\vn$ the normal
to the level surfaces, $\rhat$ in spherical geometry). In a static inviscid
fluid the linearised momentum balance for the Eulerian perturbations
$p_1,\rho_1,\Phi_1$ is
\begin{equation}
  -\nabla p_1 - \rho_1\nabla\Phi_0 - \rho_0\nabla\Phi_1 = \mathbf 0 .
  \label{eq:fluid-balance}
\end{equation}
Its tangential part on a level surface, where $\nabla\Phi_0$ has no
component and $\rho_0$ is constant, says that $p_1+\rho_0\Phi_1$ has no
tangential gradient:
\begin{equation}
  p_1 = -\rho_0\Phi_1 + f, \qquad f \text{ constant on each level surface},
  \label{eq:dahlen-p}
\end{equation}
so $f$ is a function of the level coordinate alone (of radius, in
spherical geometry). The normal part, with primes for derivatives along
$\vn$, then gives
\begin{equation}
  \rho_1 = \frac{\rho_0'}{g}\,\Phi_1 - \frac{f'}{g},
  \label{eq:dahlen-rho}
\end{equation}
and Poisson's equation in the fluid becomes
\begin{equation}
  \nabla^2\Phi_1 - 4\pi G\,\frac{\rho_0'}{g}\,\Phi_1 = -4\pi G\,\frac{f'}{g}.
  \label{eq:dahlen-poisson}
\end{equation}
The term in $\Phi_1$ is the stratification term of the Dahlen weak form
(the term (F1) of \code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, ``The CMB treatments''); the
terms in $f$ are an arbitrary one-function family. The fluid's displacement
appears nowhere: the reduction has eliminated it, and with it the fluid's
bulk constitutive law --- $\kappa$ appears nowhere either.

\paragraph{Why degrees above zero are clean.} $f$ is constant on level
surfaces, so it has no projection onto the aspherical harmonics. In every
sector $l\ge1$ the $f$-terms vanish, and the tangential balance with
Poisson's equation closes the problem; the fluid's displacement is not
needed there and is not determined (\code{pyslfp} leaves it undefined at
$l\ge1$).

\paragraph{Why degree zero is underdetermined.} At $l=0$,
\eqref{eq:dahlen-poisson} is one second-order ordinary differential
equation in radius, sourced by both $\Phi_1$ and $f'$: one equation for two
unknown functions. The interface and regularity conditions are finitely
many; they fix constants, not a function. A discretisation of the reduced
problem computes one member of the family, the one its weak form implies,
and nothing in the reduced problem marks it as the physical one. The
observables depend on the choice: $h'_0$, and $k'_0$, which mass
conservation fixes at zero in the physical problem, both move with $f$ ---
hence $h'_0=-1.457$ and $k'_0=-5.35$ on \code{fluid\_core}
(\S\ref{sec:e2-results}).

\paragraph{What selects the physical answer.} Restoring the constitutive
law. At $l=0$ the displacement is radial, $\vu=u_r(r)\,\rhat$; mass
conservation, $\rho_1 = -\nabla\cdot(\rho_0\vu) = -r^{-2}(r^2\rho_0u_r)'$,
integrates $\rho_1$ to $u_r$; and the Lagrangian pressure perturbation
$p_1 + u_r\,p_0' = -\kappa\nabla\cdot\vu$ closes the system and determines
$f$. This is the fully Lagrangian compressible treatment that
\code{pyslfp} and \code{sl3dpp} apply at degree 0, the fluid an elastic
medium with zero shear modulus. A Maxwell fluid arrives at the same place:
at long times its deviatoric stress relaxes away and leaves exactly the
$\mu=0$ compressible solid, so the Maxwell limit and the Lagrangian $l=0$
treatment coincide. The E2 measurements confirm it: the Maxwell-core route
gives $h'_0=-0.13152$ against the spectral $-0.13210$, and $k'_0=-7\times
10^{-6}$ (\S\ref{sec:e2-results}).

\paragraph{Why three dimensions have no clean static fix.} The spectral
codes escape because their degree problems decouple, so the one sector in
which the reduction fails can change formulation. A three-dimensional
discretisation has no such sector. The arbitrary family lives on functions
of the level coordinate, which no finite-element assembly isolates, and
lateral heterogeneity couples the mean sector to everything else. The
general three-dimensional answer without the hydrostatic assumption is
the Maxwell relaxation: a dissipative completion of the fluid rather
than a static formulation of it.

Two static constructions short of that exist:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{The level border.} Add $f$ as a one-dimensional unknown on
  the level foliation, which exists wherever Dahlen's method is valid
  since the background is hydrostatic, with the level-surface average of
  the Lagrangian constitutive relation as its determining equation. In
  spherical geometry it reduces to the spectral $l=0$ treatment. This is
  built (\code{doc/dahlen\_closure.tex}, \code{SetLevelBorder};
  \code{-level-border} in the drivers) and is the static route on
  hydrostatic models; it dies with the hydrostatic assumption.
\item \emph{Degree-split superposition.} For a spherical static background
  (laterally varying viscosity only), peel the load's spherical mean off
  and solve it by the one-dimensional problem. This exploits linearity and
  symmetry; it is a workaround, not a formulation, and is not used.
\end{enumerate}
The programme's position is therefore: on hydrostatic models the
closed Dahlen path is the static method at every degree; the Maxwell
relaxation is the general dissipative route and the method where the
hydrostatic assumption fails; and the spectral codes' Lagrangian
treatment is the reference at degree 0.

\section{Beside the elastic tiers: \code{AdGIA}'s internal families}
\label{sec:internal-elastic}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

These families test \code{AdGIA} alone, against \code{pyslfp} as an
independent radial reference or against exact identities. Their problems,
settings and full measurements are in \code{doc/benchmarks.tex}; what they
have established, in brief:

\paragraph{Love numbers (\code{love\_numbers/}).} Load and tidal Love
numbers of layered, self-gravitating elastic bodies, every formulation
against \code{pyslfp}, with the response also compared as fields under a cap
load (\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, ``The Love-number family''). Every
formulation agrees with \code{pyslfp} at the discretisation level on its
domain of validity: at $h=0.2$ the median relative error over degrees 1--5
is $5\times10^{-3}$--$1.4\times10^{-2}$ at order 2 and
$6\times10^{-5}$--$8\times10^{-4}$ at order 3, for every model from
\code{homogeneous} to \code{prem\_4}; the spread between formulations at
coarse resolution is mesh error, not physics. For loading at $l\ge1$ on
spherical models the Dahlen path is the cheapest and as accurate as any.
The dearer formulations buy what it cannot represent --- the compressible
fluid at degree 0, the slipping interface, the mapped machinery --- but not
non-neutral core stratification, where the general method is the Maxwell
relaxation of the fluid. The standard unmeshed-core CMB condition
(\code{uniform}) is safe for loading at $l\ge2$ ($h'$ within $0.1\,\%$,
$l'_2$ and $k'_2$ about $0.3\,\%$) and about ten times worse on tides;
buoyancy alone (\code{winkler}) is safe for neither. The fields show what the
surface numbers do not: on models with a solid inner core the order-2
displacement error is large ($0.49$ on \code{prem\_4} at $h=0.2$) where the
Love numbers are good to a per cent, through the weakly restored
translation of the inner core, and order 3 removes it. (The degree-1
frame against \code{pyslfp} is confirmed cross-code in E2.)

\paragraph{Relabelling (\code{relabelling/}).} The mapped (aspherical)
machinery, tested with the spherical physics unchanged by describing the
same problem in relabelled coordinates \cite{AlAttarCrawford2016}
(\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, ``The relabelling family''). The discrete
change-of-variables identity holds for the welded classes to
$\sim10^{-7}$ ($A=0.02$, $h=0.3$, order 2), and on the sea-level legs to
$5\times10^{-7}$ and below; the mapped Love numbers agree with the unmapped
ones at the level of the errors themselves; an aspherical reference body
gives field errors independent of the shape amplitude. Through the
slipping interface the identity does not hold: the mapped slipping forms are
not certified.

\paragraph{Perturbation (\code{perturbation/}).} Derivatives of the response
with respect to an interface radius, the mapped assembly under a
discontinuous deformation gradient against central differences of
\code{pyslfp} solves (\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, ``The perturbation
family''). On \code{fluid\_core} the degree-0 derivative agrees to
$0.8\,\%$ at order 2 and $3\times10^{-3}\,\%$ at order 3; at degrees 2 and 3
the discrepancies of 1--16\,\% are of the order of the errors of the
numbers themselves at $h=0.3$, and on \code{two\_solid} 3--17\,\%: a
consistency check at this resolution rather than a sharp verification.

\paragraph{The disc (\code{disc/}).} The two-dimensional laboratory of the
fluid treatments (\path{benchmarks/disc/README.md}; it has no section in
\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}). Without gravity a fluid disc in a solid annulus
has closed-form solutions and every tangential slip is removable, so
welded and slipping solves must coincide exactly, which tests the constraint
machinery alone. With gravity, a per-degree radial reference with welded,
free-slip and Dahlen switches measures what can be separated: on the
Adams--Williamson disc core the three agree to about $10^{-10}$; on a
uniform, non-neutral disc core the welded and free-slip solves drift within
an envelope of about $0.3\,\%$ under refinement
(\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, ``The formulations compared'').

\subsection{Manufactured solutions (\code{elastic/manufactured/})}
\label{sec:manufactured}

This family has no section in \code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, so it is written
out here; how to run it is in
\path{benchmarks/elastic/manufactured/README.md}, and the test
\path{tests/TestManufactured.cpp} gates it on canned meshes.

\paragraph{What it is.} The method of manufactured solutions for the static
self-gravitating elastic problem on a solid body
(\code{LinearQuasiStaticMixedSelfGravitatingProblem}): an exact pair
$(\vu^*,\phi^*)$ is chosen, the loads that make it the solution of the
class's weak form are derived from it symbolically, and the discrete
solution is compared with the exact one. Unlike the spherical Love-number
ladders, whose reference is another code's answer to the same physics, this
measures the discretisation alone against a closed form, on any mesh and
with variable coefficients, and gives the convergence rates in $h$ and in
the element order directly.

\paragraph{The derivation.} \path{manufacture.py} (sympy) holds the chosen
fields and the derivation and writes \path{fields.hpp}, the generated header
that the driver \code{manufactured\_benchmark} and the test both read;
nothing of the physics is typed by hand on the C++ side. With
$\mathbf{g}=\grad\Phi_0$, $\bsig = \kappa(\divg\vu)\mathbf{I} + 2\mu\,
\mathrm{d}(\vu)$ ($\mathrm{d}$ the deviatoric strain) and $\vn$ the outward
normal of the body $M$, integration by parts of the class's weak form
(\code{doc/self\_gravitation.md}) gives the loads of the two rows:
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{f} &= -\divg\bsig + \grad(\rho\,\vu\cdot\mathbf{g})
    - \divg(\rho\vu)\,\mathbf{g} + \rho\grad\phi
    && \text{in } M,\\
  \vt &= \bsig\vn && \text{on } \partial M,\\
  q &= -\frac{\nabla^2\phi}{4\pi G} - \divg(\rho\vu) \ \text{in } M,
  \qquad q = -\frac{\nabla^2\phi}{4\pi G} \ \text{in the buffer},\\
  s &= \frac{\partial_n\phi_{\mathrm{in}} - \partial_n\phi_{\mathrm{out}}}{4\pi G}
    + \rho\,\vu\cdot\vn && \text{on } \partial M.
\end{align*}
The force is the standard strong form of the pre-stressed problem. The
class assembles the \emph{symmetrised} weak form, whose Euler--Lagrange
form carries each gravity term with a half,
$\tfrac12\rho\grad(\vu\cdot\mathbf{g}) - \tfrac12\divg(\rho\vu)\,\mathbf{g}
+ \tfrac12\grad(\rho\,\vu\cdot\mathbf{g}) - \tfrac12\rho\,\mathbf{g}\,\divg\vu$,
and the natural traction $\bsig\vn + \tfrac12\rho(\vu\cdot\vn)\mathbf{g}
- \tfrac12\rho(\vu\cdot\mathbf{g})\vn$; the two differ by
$\tfrac12(\vu\cdot\mathbf{g})\grad\rho - \tfrac12(\vu\cdot\grad\rho)\,\mathbf{g}$
in the body and by $\tfrac12\rho[(\vu\cdot\vn)\mathbf{g} -
(\vu\cdot\mathbf{g})\vn]$ on the surface, both zero on a hydrostatic
background ($\grad\rho \parallel \mathbf{g}$, and $\mathbf{g} \parallel
\vn$ on a level surface), and identically here. The derivation works from
the weak form, so the sources match the assembled equations whatever the
background. Three choices keep the test clean. The potential is exactly harmonic in the
buffer, a sum of exterior multipoles about the centre, so the buffer
carries no source and the DtN condition holds exactly whatever the buffer's
radius; inside the body it is the interior continuation of the multipoles'
traces plus $(a^2-r^2)\,w(\vx)$, continuous across the surface with a kink
that $s$ carries. The background is the self-consistent potential of the
homogeneous ball (quadratic inside), so the body is in hydrostatic
equilibrium and the rigid modes are exact null pairs of the continuous
problem, as the solver's projection assumes; the driver fits the rigid part
of $\vu_h-\vu^*$ on the discrete rigid modes and removes it, with its
partner $-\mathbf{r}\cdot\grad\Phi_0$ from the potential (and in 2-D the
constant the class projects out). The moduli vary smoothly
($\kappa = 1+0.2xy$, $\mu = 0.5+0.1z^2$) and the displacement is
trigonometric and exponential, so no element order represents the solution
exactly. Units are those of the tests: unit radius, $G=0.05$, $\rho=1$,
$\rho g a/\mu\approx0.4$ at the surface.

\paragraph{Conditions.} Meshes from \path{meshes/homogeneous_body.py}
(order-2 geometry, buffer to 1.5 radii, element size $2h$ in the far
field), MINRES to $10^{-11}$, relative $L^2$ errors over the ball of $\vu$,
of the stress $\bsig(\vu)$ and of $\phi$, with the rates between
consecutive sizes. On a smooth solution the
expected rates are $p+1$ for $\vu$ and $\phi$ and $p$ for the stress, down
to the floor set by the order-2 geometry and the solver tolerance.

\paragraph{Finding 1: order 1 does not converge on curved geometry.} In
2-D the displacement error is $4.9\times10^{-2}$, $4.6\times10^{-2}$ and
$4.5\times10^{-2}$ at $h=0.1$, $0.05$, $0.025$; in 3-D it falls slowly
(rates 1.4 and 0.7). The rigid rotations of an order-2 geometry are not in
the order-1 space (\code{doc/self\_gravitation.md}, ``Element order on
curved meshes''): the displacement order must be at least that of the
geometry.

\paragraph{Finding 2: the background gravity and the potential order.} The
class projects $\Phi_0$ onto the potential space and differentiates it; the
gradient's $O(h^p)$ error enters the gravity terms, and with the potential
at the displacement's order the displacement's $L^2$ rate is $p$, not
$p+1$ (2-D: 2.1 at order 2, 3.0 at order 3). With the potential space one
order higher (the drivers' \code{-ophi}; \code{run --phi-offset 1}) the
rate is $p+1$ (order 2: 3.0). The potential converges at $p+1$ either way.

\paragraph{Finding 3: the nodal interpolant caps the energy norm at second
order.} The term $\tfrac12\rho\,\grad(\vu\cdot\grad\Phi_0)\cdot\vu'$ was
assembled by \code{DomainVectorGradVectorIntegrator}, which differentiates
the nodal interpolant of $\vu\cdot\grad\Phi_0$ on the trial space; in the
symmetrised form the interpolant is applied to the oscillatory test
functions as well, where its error is $O(h^2)$ at any order. The stress
therefore converges at rate 2 at orders 2, 3 and 4 alike (2-D, potential one
order up: 1.9, 2.0, 2.1), the displacement one or two orders better by
duality. The zero-density problem is clean (order 3: $\vu$ 3.9, stress
3.0), and so is the gravitating one once the integrator uses the product
rule $\grad(\vu\cdot\grad\Phi_0) = (\grad\vu)^T\grad\Phi_0 +
(\grad\grad\Phi_0)\,\vu$ with the Hessian of $\Phi_0$ supplied: an analytic
one (\code{SetBackgroundHessian}, driver \code{-exact-hessian}) or that of
the discrete $\Phi_0$ itself (\code{UseDiscreteBackgroundHessian}, driver
\code{-discrete-hessian}), which give identical results with the potential
one order up. Table~\ref{tab:manufactured-2d} compares the two routes at

\section{Tier V: viscoelastic loading without the sea-level equation}
\label{sec:v}

\status{V1, the Maxwell load Love-number histories of
gravitating models without rotation, is done in all three codes, with
measurements on \code{homogeneous}, \code{homogeneous\_lithosphere} and
\code{fluid\_core} (\S\ref{sec:v1}). The spectral pair agrees to
$1.3\times10^{-11}$, and both spectral codes sit within $1.6\times10^{-4}$
of the Laplace-domain reference, the stepping error that the three legs
share; \code{AdGIA}'s Dahlen path at $h=0.3$ agrees with \code{pyslfp} to
$1.0$--$2.3\times10^{-2}$ at order 2 and $5.2$--$8.2\times10^{-4}$ at
order 3, degree 1 in the centre-of-mass frame included.}

\paragraph{The problem.} The response of spherical models to prescribed
surface-load histories, with no sea-level equation, in all three codes,
with the same splits as the elastic tiers: the model ladder; gravity off and
on; rotational feedback off and on. The three codes share the
exponential-trapezoid step weights, so with matched steps a comparison
measures the spatial discretisation.

\paragraph{References.} The spectral codes, against each other at tier
(i) where they solve the identical discrete problem. On the non-gravitating
rung the E1 reference extends exactly: by the correspondence principle the
transfer functions $U(a;s)$, $V(a;s)$ are rational in the Laplace variable,
which is how the viscoelastic sphere sub-family's exact histories are built.
Semi-analytical references (modal sums, AAA with Talbot, Gaver--Stehfest)
are kept as secondary.

\paragraph{The lateral pair (V-lat).} \code{AdGIA} and \code{sl3dpp} are the
two codes with laterally varying viscosity. With a radial elastic structure
and viscosity $\bar\eta(r)\,e^{\delta(r,\theta,\varphi)}$, the two can be
compared for loading without sea level, the viscosity field exchanged as
netCDF.

\subsection{Tier V1: Maxwell Love-number histories}
\label{sec:v1}

\paragraph{The problem and the compared quantities.} V1 is gravitating, without rotation and without the
sea-level equation:
the conventional load numbers of E2 (\S\ref{sec:e2-problem}) as histories,
$h'_l(t)$, $l'_l(t)$, $k'_l(t)$ for degrees 0--4, under a unit Heaviside
surface load of each degree switched on at $t=0$. Every solid layer is a
Maxwell body with one Maxwell time ($\tau=\infty$ keeps a layer elastic); a
fluid core relaxes nothing. The histories are compared at the 13 geometric
output times of the Love-history sub-family (\S\ref{sec:internal-visco}),
\begin{equation}
  t_k = 0.011\cdot2^{5k/6}\,\tau_{\min},\qquad k=0,\dots,12,
  \label{eq:v1-times}
\end{equation}
from $0.011$ to $11.264\,\tau_{\min}$, rounded to six decimals in units of
$\tau_{\min}$, the shortest Maxwell time. The exchange file has one table
per model and code: the time \code{t}, in the same unit, followed by
\code{hp<l> lp<l> kp<l>} for each degree. Only $t/\tau$ enters the
quasi-static problem, so the spectral codes run $\tau=10^{11}$\,s and
\code{AdGIA} $\tau=1$ in its own time unit, and the numbers are the same.
The models are \code{homogeneous} (one Maxwell layer, $\tau=10^{11}$\,s),
\code{homogeneous\_lithosphere} (the interior Maxwell with
$\tau=10^{11}$\,s, the outer shell elastic) and \code{fluid\_core} (the
mantle Maxwell with $\tau=10^{11}$\,s), all at $l_{\max}=4$. Degree 1 is in
the centre-of-mass frame, as in E2; degree 0 is compared except on
\code{AdGIA}'s Dahlen path on \code{fluid\_core}, where it is left out by
design (\S\ref{sec:e2-degree0}).

\paragraph{The stepping rule.} All three legs step with the exponential
trapezoid, in equal steps between consecutive output times, at least 4 in
each interval and none longer than $0.25\,\tau_{\min}$: 79 steps to
$11.264\,\tau_{\min}$ (4 in each interval to $t_9\approx1.99\,\tau_{\min}$,
then 7, 12 and 20). The three codes share the step-weight algebra
(\S\ref{sec:codes}), so on the same steps their differences are spatial.
The two spectral codes also share the uniform $l_{\max}$ mesh rule
(\S\ref{sec:models}) and so solve the identical discrete problem in space
and time, tolerance tier (i); \code{AdGIA}'s difference from either is its
finite-element discretisation, tier (iii). The sub-family's Laplace-domain
reference (\path{laplace_reference.py}: the correspondence principle on
\code{pyslfp}'s elastic solves, Gaver--Stehfest inversion;
\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, ``The Laplace-domain reference'') takes no
steps, and is the secondary check of the stepping the three legs share.

\paragraph{The spectral pair.} On all three models \code{sl3dpp} and
\code{pyslfp} agree to about $10^{-11}$ at every time and degree, the worst
entry in $k'_0$, within the $10^{-10}$ gate of tolerance tier (i). Unlike E2's spectral comparison,
which set the graded mesh against the uniform rule, this is the identical
discrete problem in space and time --- the same model (the deck is the
model to rounding, \S\ref{sec:models}) on the same rule, stepped with the
same weights on the same steps --- so the agreement checks the two
viscoelastic implementations against each other with nothing else left to
differ. It includes degree 0 on \code{fluid\_core} under relaxation, which
both codes treat with the compressible Lagrangian equations
(\S\ref{sec:degrees01}).

\paragraph{The Laplace check.} Against the Laplace reference both spectral
codes sit at $1.5$--$1.6\times10^{-4}$ on every model, in $h'_3$ or $h'_4$,
with $h'_2$ at $1.0$--$1.5\times10^{-4}$, and $l'$, $k'$ and degrees 0 and 1
at most $9\times10^{-5}$. The two codes being $10^{-11}$ apart, this is one
measurement made twice: the time-discretisation error of the shared rule.
The sub-family's own stepping refinement, 40 steps per interval and
$\Delta t\le0.025\,\tau_{\min}$ on \code{homogeneous}, brings it down to
the accuracy of the Gaver--Stehfest inversion itself, about
$3\times10^{-5}$ (measured on a different scale); the stepping error
common to the three legs is therefore of order $10^{-4}$ at the default
rule. It is below \code{AdGIA}'s differences at
both orders, and it largely cancels from them, since \code{AdGIA} takes
the same steps with the same weights. The misfit is thus mostly the
spectral codes' own stepping error, and the floor of about
$3\times10^{-5}$ that remains under refinement, like the instability
horizon beyond which the reference marks its times invalid, is where the
Gaver--Stehfest inversion stops: these residuals are the limitation of the
method, not information about the codes. The programme's position: the
Laplace route is good enough to confirm that the shared
stepping is sound at the $10^{-4}$ level, and no better; the reference for
the time-domain problem is the spectral pair, which agrees to $10^{-11}$ on
the identical discrete problem; and the semi-analytical references stay
secondary, as set out for the tier (\S\ref{sec:v}, ``References'').

\paragraph{\code{AdGIA}.} The Dahlen path agrees with \code{pyslfp} at the
discretisation level of $h=0.3$ on all three models: worst
$1.0$--$2.3\times10^{-2}$ at order 2 and $5.2$--$8.2\times10^{-4}$ at
order 3, a factor of 44, 13 and 19 better on \code{homogeneous},
\code{homogeneous\_lithosphere} and \code{fluid\_core}. Which channel is
worst depends on the model and the order. On \code{homogeneous} at order 2
it is $h'_2$ ($2.3\times10^{-2}$), followed by $k'_2$ ($1.4\times10^{-2}$),
$l'_2$ and $l'_4$ ($1.3\times10^{-2}$), $l'_3$ ($9.3\times10^{-3}$) and
$h'_4$ ($8.7\times10^{-3}$); at order 3 it is $h'_4$ ($5.2\times10^{-4}$),
followed by $l'_4$ ($4.0\times10^{-4}$) and $l'_2$ ($3.0\times10^{-4}$),
with $h'_2$ down to $5.1\times10^{-5}$. On \code{homogeneous\_lithosphere}
it is $h'_4$ ($1.0\times10^{-2}$; then $h'_3$ $4.5\times10^{-3}$, $k'_4$
$4.4\times10^{-3}$) at order 2 and $h'_2$ ($7.9\times10^{-4}$; then $k'_2$
$4.7\times10^{-4}$, $h'_3$ $4.6\times10^{-4}$) at order 3. On
\code{fluid\_core} at order 2 it is the degree-1 tangential number $l'_1$
($1.5\times10^{-2}$), followed by $h'_2$ ($1.1\times10^{-2}$), $h'_4$
($8.9\times10^{-3}$) and $l'_4$ ($6.3\times10^{-3}$); at order 3, $l'_2$
($8.2\times10^{-4}$), then $l'_1$ ($5.8\times10^{-4}$) and $h'_3$
($4.2\times10^{-4}$). The grade is comparable to that of E2's elastic numbers at $h=0.3$
(\S\ref{sec:e2-results}) and to the sub-family's SDIRK23 histories against
the Laplace reference (\S\ref{sec:internal-visco}).

\paragraph{Along the history.} Where in time the error sits is read from
the sub-family's own comparer (\path{viscoelastic/love/compare.py}, against
the Laplace reference), which judges each channel on its own scale and
separates the elastic error at $t=0^+$ from the error of the relaxation
increment $f(t)-f(0)$, the latter relative to the reference's drift. On
\code{homogeneous} at order 2, $h'_2$ is $3.9\times10^{-3}$ from the
reference at $t=0^+$ but $2.3\times10^{-2}$ worst over the history, and its
relaxation increment is off by $3.5\times10^{-2}$ of the drift; at order 3
the three coincide ($1.4\times10^{-4}$, $1.6\times10^{-4}$,
$1.6\times10^{-4}$). At order 2 the error therefore grows along the
history: $h'_2$ goes from $-0.65$ to $-2.00$ by $11.3\,\tau_{\min}$
($-2.04$ at order 2), and it is the late, largely viscous response, by then
three times the elastic one, that $h=0.3$ at order 2 under-resolves. At
order 3 the relative error is the elastic one throughout. The exchange
comparison shows the same on the degree's scale, which for $h'_2$ is the
JSON comparer's: at order 2 the $h'_2$ entry rises from about $10^{-3}$ at
the first time to $2.3\times10^{-2}$ at the last, the worst entry on
\code{homogeneous}, while $h'_4$ stays between $5\times10^{-3}$ and
$9\times10^{-3}$. The worst order-2 entry on \code{fluid\_core}, $l'_1$,
grows the same way, from $1.7\times10^{-3}$ at the first time to
$1.5\times10^{-2}$ at the last as $l'_1$ goes from $-0.89$ to $-1.34$, and
order 3 brings it to $5.8\times10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{Degrees 0 and 1.} $k'_1=-1$ to rounding ($10^{-15}$) at every
time in all three codes: the centre-of-mass frame conventions that E2
confirmed elastically hold for the viscoelastic histories too. $h'_1$ and
$l'_1$ agree with \code{pyslfp} to $4\times10^{-4}$--$1.5\times10^{-2}$ at
order 2 and $2\times10^{-5}$--$6\times10^{-4}$ at order 3; the largest,
$l'_1$ on \code{fluid\_core} at order 2, is the late-history growth just
described. Degree 0 on the solid models, where the Dahlen path has no fluid
to reduce, agrees on its own scale to $5.8\times10^{-4}$
(\code{homogeneous}) and $3.8\times10^{-3}$
(\code{homogeneous\_lithosphere}) in $h'_0$ at order 2, and
$2.4\times10^{-4}$ and $6.8\times10^{-5}$ at order 3. On \code{fluid\_core} it is left out, the
known Dahlen defect of \S\ref{sec:e2-degree0}.

\paragraph{Cost.} Each run is 79 stepping solves and one observation solve.
On 8 ranks: 11, 19 and 29\,s at order 2 on \code{homogeneous},
\code{homogeneous\_lithosphere} and \code{fluid\_core}, and 38, 133 and
178\,s at order 3, so that order 3 costs 3.5 to 7 times order 2 for a
factor of 13 to 44 in accuracy.

The Maxwell times follow two conventions. \code{viscoelastic\_love}, like
\code{laplace\_reference}, takes one per \emph{solid} layer: \code{-tau
1,inf} for \code{homogeneous\_lithosphere}, \code{-tau 1} for
\code{fluid\_core} (there with \code{-lmin 1}). The spectral drivers take
one per layer, the fluid included: \code{1e11,inf} for
\code{homogeneous\_lithosphere} and \code{inf,1e11} for \code{fluid\_core}.

\paragraph{Output conventions.} \code{viscoelastic\_love}'s default
output times are rounded as the reference writes them (six decimals), so
that the exchange comparer keys on the \code{t} column; the sub-family's
own comparer skips a channel that the reference leaves identically zero
($l'_0$).

\section{Beside tier V: the viscoelastic family}
\label{sec:internal-visco}

\code{AdGIA}'s viscoelastic family (\path{viscoelastic/},
\code{doc/benchmarks.tex}, ``The viscoelastic family'') has four
sub-families. The box and sphere sub-families are non-gravitating and
solid-only, with references exact to about $10^{-10}$, so any mismatch is
the code's: on them every integrator reaches its design order on every body
it is stable on, with one finding beyond the design orders --- the
exponential trapezoid loses half an order (1.5 against 2) under stress
control on a stiff multi-branch body; and order-2 elements converge at
about fourth order in the surface coefficients on curved spherical meshes.
The sphere sub-family's free layered balls under a degree-$l$ pressure are
the viscoelastic version of E1, and its laterally varying viscosity runs
test the integrators against a much smaller step. The stepping survey
compares the schemes on one beam. The Maxwell Love-number histories of
gravitating models (\path{viscoelastic/love/}) are compared with
\code{pyslfp} through the correspondence principle and Gaver--Stehfest
inversion: history errors of $0.5$--$1.1\times10^{-2}$ in $h'$ at order 2 on
\code{fluid\_core} ($h=0.3$) and $1.7$--$3.2\times10^{-4}$ at order 3, the
errors spatial, not temporal. The same sub-family is the home of V1
(\S\ref{sec:v1}), its cross-code form: the histories stepped in the time
domain by all three codes under one stepping rule, with the Laplace
reference as the secondary check. The choice of integrator these support is
described in \code{doc/viscoelasticity.md}.

\section{Tier S: sea level}
\label{sec:s}

\status{The cross-code tier is not run. An
\code{AdGIA}--\code{pyslfp} comparison (\S\ref{sec:internal-sea}) and

\paragraph{The problem.} The sea-level equation on the same model
hierarchy: elastic fingerprints first, three-way, with and without
rotational feedback and shoreline migration; then full viscoelastic GIA
runs; and 3-D viscosity with the two codes that have it. All three codes
solve the generalised sea-level equation of \code{pyslfp}, of which
\code{sl3dpp}'s is a port and \code{AdGIA}'s monolithic treatment a
reformulation.

\section{Beside tier S: the sea-level family}
\label{sec:internal-sea}

\code{AdGIA}'s monolithic sea-level solve (\code{SetWaterLoad}) against
\code{pyslfp}'s pseudo-spectral solution of the same problem, with
rotation, shoreline-migration and timing legs and a real-geography variant
on coastline-refined meshes (\path{benchmarks/sea_level/README.md}; the
family has no section in \code{doc/benchmarks.tex}). At toy resolution
(\code{homogeneous}, $h=0.4$, order 2, $l_{\max}=16$) the ocean-RMS
relative difference is $1.1\,\%$ on the Dahlen path and $1.2\,\%$ on the
welded referential class, the floor of two discretisations at this
resolution rather than a disagreement. Love-number agreement is assumed there and tested by
the Love-number family, which is why the elastic tiers come first.
